Where the data comes from

Everything comes from the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit's own website: its opinions and Rule 36 judgments (as PDFs), its postings of oral-argument recordings, and its monthly argument calendars. The archive holds … decisions posted from 2015 through …: … written opinions and … Rule 36 judgments (affirmances without opinion). Coverage starts in 2015 because the court's online index is essentially complete from that year and incomplete before it. All case types are included, not only patent cases.

What is read by text rules

These facts are read from each PDF by fixed text rules, not by AI, so the same document always gives the same result:

  • Panel (the "Before …" line), author ("Opinion for the court filed by …", or per curiam), and separate opinions.
  • Decision date, precedential status, and origin (district court, PTAB, ITC, MSPB, Court of Federal Claims, Veterans Court, and so on).
  • Pro se parties, from the list of counsel at the top of the opinion.
  • Argument date: the date the court posted the argument recording, matched by appeal number. Recordings are normally posted the same day. A few days in 2013–2015 when the court posted hundreds of old recordings at once give no usable date; those cases count as argued but are left out of timing figures.

Judges who are not members of the Federal Circuit (district judges and others sitting by designation) are grouped as "visiting judges"; there is too little data to rate them one by one.

What the AI codes

Each written opinion (… in all) was read in full by an AI model (…). From a fixed list of 70 issue types (listed below), it recorded every issue the court actually decided and, for each one:

  • which side the ruling favored, or "mixed" (some claims, patents, or counts each way), or "unclear";
  • the court's disposition (affirmed, reversed, vacated, and so on);
  • which judges, if any, dissented on that issue;
  • a one-sentence description and a supporting quote from the opinion, shown when you click a row on the dashboard.

… issues were coded. Rules the AI was given:

  • Only issues the court decided are coded. Issues the court "need not reach" are not.
  • A vacatur or remand favors the side that appealed, on the issue actually decided. If the court sent a case back only because the tribunal failed to explain itself, only that procedural issue is coded, not the merits.
  • Claim construction is coded by its effect on the judgment: a construction that leads the court to vacate a noninfringement judgment favors the patent owner. When the effect is unclear, it is coded "unclear".
  • When the court refuses to consider an argument as forfeited, that is coded as a forfeiture ruling for the side that benefits, not as a ruling on the underlying issue.
  • The court's routine statement of its own jurisdiction is not coded; jurisdiction is coded only when it was disputed.

Sides

Votes are shown by the side they favored, in the issue's own terms (for example "nonobvious (patent owner)"), never as "appellant won", because either side can be the appellant. Sides are fixed for each case type:

How votes are counted

  • Every judge on the panel gets one vote on every coded issue, including judges who joined a unanimous opinion.
  • A judge who dissented on an issue is counted for the other side on that issue only. A partial dissent counts only for the issues it covers; a concurrence counts with the majority. A dissent on a mixed or unclear ruling counts as unclear.
  • Percentages are shares of votes for one side, mixed, and the other side. "Unclear" votes are shown in brackets, e.g. "(+3)", and left out of the percentages.
  • By default a judge's record is shown only in the same case type as the case (for example, obviousness in PTAB appeals separately from obviousness in district-court appeals). Untick "Only this case type" to combine them.
  • Below each issue, the same count is shown for the issue's whole group (for example all "Patent validity" issues), as a fallback when the issue alone has few votes.
  • Rule 36 judgments give no reasons, so they are left out of issue votes. Vote figures describe cases with written opinions.

Comparing a judge with the court

Next to each judge, the dashboard shows the whole court's rulings on the same issue under the same filters (one count per ruling, not per judge), and a tick on the judge's bar marks the court's share.

  • A judge is said to have voted one way "more often" or "less often" than the court only when a 95% confidence interval (Wilson) around the judge's share leaves out the court's share. Otherwise the judge is "within the court's range".
  • Judges with fewer than 10 votes on the issue are shown in grey and not compared.
  • With about 20 judges and 70 issues, some differences will appear by chance. Read them together with the cases behind them (click any row).
  • A judge's own votes are part of the court's figure, which makes differences slightly harder to find, not easier.

Sitting together, authorship, timing, and Rule 36

  • Sitting together: the number of decisions (written opinions and Rule 36 judgments) whose panel included both judges, or all three. Consolidated appeals decided together count once. Two-judge panels (when a judge died or recused) count for pairs, not for all three.
  • Wrote the opinion: of the written panel opinions a judge sat on, the share the judge signed. The court figure is signed opinions divided by panel seats, which is a little under one in three because of per curiam opinions.
  • Days to opinion: the median number of days from argument to a signed opinion the judge wrote, shown separately for precedential and nonprecedential opinions, only for cases with a known argument date.
  • Rule 36 rate: Rule 36 judgments as a share of all decisions in cases the judge sat on. When a case is selected, only cases from the same origin and with the same argued / on-the-briefs status are counted.

What is left out

  • En banc decisions: kept out of judge statistics (a separate group).
  • Revised opinions: when the court reissues a revised or corrected opinion, the original is replaced and left out.
  • Decisions whose PDF names no panel (about 1%, mostly unsigned per curiam opinions in pro se cases and a January 2019 batch of Rule 36 judgments): left out of judge statistics, but counted in whole-court figures.
  • Pro se cases: left out by default (you can include them with the checkbox). Without this filter, jurisdictional dismissals in pro se veterans, employment, and Court of Federal Claims appeals would dominate those records.
  • Judge Stark's panels before 2022, when he sat by designation as a district judge, are in the visiting group, not his Federal Circuit record.

Accuracy check

25 issues drawn at random from patent cases were each checked against the opinion by a person; the AI had coded all 25 correctly. A check of this size cannot rule out occasional errors, and other case types were not checked.

The model was chosen after a 50-case test in which two AI models coded the same opinions. The models agreed on the side favored in 73 of 73 issues they both coded, and on the panel and author in 50 of 50 cases.

Known limits

  • AI coding has some errors. Every number links to its cases, with the AI's description and a quote, so it can be checked.
  • The AI sometimes splits one ruling into two issues, or adds a minor procedural ruling another reader would skip. Whether it misses issues was not separately checked.
  • About 4% of issues fit none of the 70 types ("Other"). These are mostly non-patent claims in patent cases (trade secrets, contract, and antitrust) and settlement-agreement disputes.
  • The pro se flag does not say which side was pro se, and it counts a lawyer representing themselves as pro se.
  • The argument date is the date the recording was posted, which is normally, but not always, the argument date.
  • The panel shown on the dashboard is entered by the user. The court posts the actual panel on its daily schedule about one hour before argument.

Updates

The argument calendar is re-read every morning. New decisions are added every week, read by the same text rules and coded by the same AI model with the same instructions. If the AI coding is ever redone with a different model, the dashboard will say so and name the new version.
